An above-ground pool costs $1,500 to $15,000 fully installed; an inground pool averages around $66,000, with a total range of roughly $14,000 to $135,000-plus depending on material and features. Above-ground pools last 7 to 20 years and rarely add resale value. Inground pools last decades, fiberglass shells 25 to 50 years, concrete 50-plus, and can return real value at resale in warm-climate markets.

The gap isn't just the sticker price. It's what each pool is actually built to do: an above-ground pool is a seasonal, replaceable structure, while an inground pool is a permanent addition to the property.

## How much does each pool type cost installed?

| | Above-ground | Inground |
|---|---|---|
| Installed cost | $1,500 to $15,000-plus | $14,000 to $135,000-plus (avg. around $66,000) |
| Unit-only cost | $1,000 to $6,000 | Not applicable, built on-site |

(Figures from [NerdWallet's pool cost breakdown](https://www.nerdwallet.com/home-ownership/home-improvement/learn/inground-pool-cost) and [Swimply's above-ground pool cost guide](https://swimply.com/hostblog/post/above-ground-pool-cost-the-complete-price-guide-2026), 2026.) The inground range is wide because it spans a basic vinyl liner pool at the low end up to a custom gunite pool with a spa, tanning ledge, and water features at the high end; material choice alone (vinyl, fiberglass, or concrete/gunite) typically swings the price by tens of thousands of dollars.

## How long does each type actually last?

Above-ground pools generally last 7 to 20 years, and the liner inside one needs replacing every 5 to 9 years regardless of how long the structure itself holds up. Inground pools last much longer by design: fiberglass shells run 25 to 50-plus years, and well-maintained concrete or gunite pools can exceed 50 years. That lifespan gap is the core trade-off: above-ground buys years of use for a fraction of the cost, inground buys decades.

## Does either pool type add resale value?

Inground pools can meaningfully add value, particularly in warm-climate, high-demand markets like Florida, Arizona, and parts of California, where some sources cite a 7 to 15% home value increase and inground pools returning roughly 50 to 70% of their cost at resale ([HFS Financial, pool value guide](https://www.hfsfinancial.net/blog/how-much-value-does-a-pool-add-to-a-home/); [PoolGuardUSA](https://poolguardusa.com/how-much-value-does-a-pool-add-to-a-house/)). Above-ground pools typically add little to no resale value and are frequently removed before a home goes on the market, since buyers tend to see them as a temporary structure rather than a permanent feature.

In cooler-climate states, Illinois, Oregon, Minnesota, and much of the Northeast, even an inground pool may add little value and can be viewed by some buyers and appraisers as a maintenance liability rather than an asset ([PoolGuardUSA](https://poolguardusa.com/how-much-value-does-a-pool-add-to-a-house/)). Climate and local buyer expectations matter more to resale value than the pool itself.

## How different is the actual installation process?

An above-ground pool can often go from delivery to swimmable in a matter of days, sometimes a single weekend for a basic kit pool with a level site already prepped. An inground pool is a construction project: excavation, structural work (vinyl liner walls, a fiberglass shell drop-in, or poured gunite), plumbing, decking, and curing time, especially for concrete and gunite, which typically stretches the timeline to 8 to 12 weeks or more from groundbreaking to first swim. That timeline difference is one of the most underweighted factors in the above-ground versus inground decision: an inground pool ordered in spring may not be ready until well into summer.

## What actually costs more to own, year to year?

Trade sources are directionally consistent here but don't publish a hard dollar comparison, so treat this as a general pattern rather than a fixed number: inground pools tend to cost more to maintain because they typically hold more water, run larger pumps and filtration systems, and have more surface area for algae and debris to accumulate on. Above-ground pools are usually smaller in volume, which means less chemical and less energy to filter and heat, but they carry their own recurring cost in liner replacement every 5 to 9 years, a cost inground vinyl pools also share but concrete and fiberglass pools mostly avoid.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is an above-ground pool cheaper than an inground pool long-term?**
Almost always, yes. Above-ground pools cost a fraction of inground installation and have lower ongoing chemical and energy costs due to smaller water volume, though they need liner replacement every 5 to 9 years and don't last as long overall.

**Does an inground pool add more home value than an above-ground pool?**
Generally yes, particularly in warm-climate markets where inground pools can return 50 to 70% of their cost. Above-ground pools typically add little to no resale value and are often removed before a sale.

**How long does it take to install each type?**
An above-ground pool can be ready to swim in days, sometimes a single weekend. An inground pool typically takes 8 to 12 weeks or more from groundbreaking to first swim, largely due to excavation, structural work, and curing time for concrete or gunite.

**Which pool type costs less to maintain?**
Above-ground pools generally cost less to maintain due to smaller water volume and simpler equipment. Inground pools cost more to run but last significantly longer, so the real comparison is cost per year of ownership, not just the annual number alone.

Bottom line: above-ground buys years of swimming for a fraction of the cost; inground buys decades and, in the right market, some of that cost back at resale. Pick based on how long you'll own the property, not just the pool.
