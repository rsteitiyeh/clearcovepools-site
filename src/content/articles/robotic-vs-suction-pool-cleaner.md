A robotic pool cleaner costs $500 to $1,500 or more upfront versus $200 to $500 for a suction cleaner, but a government-tested energy study found robotic cleaners use just 197 kWh a year versus 1,675 kWh for a filter-pump-powered suction cleaner, a gap large enough that the robot often wins on total cost within a few years despite the higher sticker price ([Pacific Gas and Electric Company residential pool cleaner study, cited via ENERGY STAR](https://www.energystar.gov/products/pool_pumps)).

The upfront price is the number every buying guide leads with. The energy draw, and what each cleaner does to your existing pump and filter, is the number that actually decides which one costs less by year three.

## How does energy use compare between robotic and suction cleaners?

| Cleaner type | Annual energy use | How it's powered |
|---|---|---|
| Robotic | 197 kWh/year | Independent low-voltage transformer, plugs into a wall outlet |
| Suction (filter-pump-powered) | 1,675 kWh/year | Draws on your pool's main circulation pump |
| Pressure-side (booster-pump-powered) | 2,989 kWh/year | Needs a dedicated booster pump in addition to the main pump |

Figures come from a Pacific Gas and Electric Company laboratory test of residential pool cleaners, cited by the EPA's ENERGY STAR program ([ENERGY STAR, Pool Pumps, citing PG&E Report #0918](https://www.energystar.gov/products/pool_pumps)). A suction cleaner isn't a separate appliance with its own power draw; it works by running your main pump longer and harder than filtration alone requires, which is why its "energy use" figure is so much higher than a robot's own small motor.

## What does that actually cost per year, and over five years?

At the national average residential electricity rate of roughly $0.16 per kWh, the PG&E figures translate to about $32 a year for a robotic cleaner versus $268 a year for a suction cleaner running off the main pump.

**Worked example, 5-year total cost of ownership:**

| Cleaner type | Upfront cost | 5-year electricity | 5-year total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Robotic | $500 to $1,500 | ~$158 | $658 to $1,658 |
| Suction | $200 to $500 | ~$1,340 | $1,540 to $1,840 |

This simple comparison leaves out repair costs, which cut the other way for each type: a suction cleaner's own parts (diaphragms, hoses) are cheap, but the extra runtime it forces on your main pump and filter adds wear those systems weren't sized for. A robotic cleaner's parts (drive belts, filter cartridges, occasionally a motor) cost more individually but don't touch your pool's primary equipment at all.

## Does a robotic cleaner actually clean better, or just cheaper to run?

Robotic cleaners typically finish a full clean cycle in 1.5 to 2 hours using independent navigation and their own onboard filter, while suction cleaners commonly take 4 to 6 hours to fully cover an average 20,000-gallon pool since they rely on your main pump's flow rate and move in simpler, less directed patterns ([Dreame, pool cleaner operating cost comparison, 2025](https://pool.dreametech.com/blogs/blog/pool-cleaner-operating-costs-robotic-vs-suction-vs-pressure-cleaners-compared)). Robots also commonly climb walls and clean the waterline, a job suction cleaners generally can't do since they depend on the pool's return flow to move and stay limited to the floor.

## What's the real tradeoff with suction cleaners?

Suction cleaners are the lowest upfront cost and the simplest mechanically, connecting directly to a dedicated suction line or the skimmer. The tradeoff is that every hour they run is an hour your main pump runs too, at full flow, which is exactly the demand a variable-speed pump's energy savings depend on avoiding. Running a suction cleaner daily effectively cancels out a meaningful chunk of whatever a variable-speed pump saves you elsewhere, since the pump has to spend that circulation time at higher speed to generate suction.

## Which brands make which type, and does that matter?

Suction and pressure-side cleaners are the traditional category, sold by brands like Pentair (Kreepy Krauly) and Hayward (Navigator, PoolVac). Robotic cleaners are made by Dolphin (Maytronics), Polaris (Zodiac, which also sells pressure-side units), and several newer entrants. Polaris is the one brand that spans both categories, selling both traditional pressure-side cleaners and robotic models, which makes it a useful place to directly compare a manufacturer's own pricing across both types before deciding.

Whichever cleaner runs longer on your pool, check your chemistry more often for the first few weeks after switching. ## Frequently asked questions

**Is a robotic pool cleaner worth the extra cost?**
For most pools run regularly, yes. The energy savings alone, roughly $236 a year at national average electricity rates, typically closes most or all of the upfront price gap within three to five years, before counting the independent filtration and wall-climbing capability a suction cleaner doesn't have.

**Do suction cleaners work as well as robotic cleaners?**
For floor debris on a regularly maintained pool, suction cleaners do an adequate job. They generally can't climb walls or clean the waterline, and their reliance on the main pump means cleaning quality is tied to how well that pump and filter are already running.

**Can a robotic cleaner replace my pool's main pump and filter?**
No. A robotic cleaner has its own separate filter for debris it picks up, but it doesn't sanitize or circulate the full body of water the way your main pump and filtration system does. The two systems do different jobs and both are still needed.

**Which is better for above-ground pools, suction or robotic?**
Robotic cleaners work in above-ground pools and are increasingly the default recommendation, since above-ground pumps are often smaller and suction cleaners add proportionally more strain to that smaller system. Cordless or low-voltage robotic models sized for smaller pools are widely available.

Bottom line: suction cleaners cost less to buy but push their energy cost onto your main pump, running roughly 1,675 kWh a year versus a robot's 197 kWh. For most pools run several times a week, that gap closes the price difference within a few years, before counting the independent filtration and wall-climbing a robot adds.
