A salt water pool is a chlorine pool. The salt cell uses electrolysis to convert dissolved salt (typically maintained at 3,000 to 4,000 ppm) into hypochlorous acid, the exact same active sanitizer that liquid chlorine, tablets, and granular shock produce ([Discount Salt Pool, salt chlorine generator guide](https://www.discountsaltpool.com/what-do-you-need-to-know-about-salt-chlorine-generators-complete-guide-for-future-salt-pool-owners)). The real decision isn't "chlorine or no chlorine," it's upfront cost and maintenance style: a salt system costs $1,700 to $2,500 to install and needs a cell replacement every 3 to 7 years at $700 to $1,100, while traditional chlorine needs no special equipment but means buying sanitizer by the bucket all season.

Both systems are governed by the same water chemistry science and the same CDC guidance, because chemically, the water a swimmer is standing in is identical. What differs is how the chlorine gets there.

## Is a salt water pool actually chlorine-free?

No, and this is the single most misunderstood fact in pool ownership. Inside the salt cell, an electric current passes dissolved sodium chloride (table salt, NaCl) across titanium plates, splitting it into its components; the chlorine portion reacts with water to form hypochlorous acid (HOCl) and sodium hypochlorite, which is chemically identical to the active sanitizer in bottled liquid chlorine ([CircuPool, common salt cell questions](https://www.circupool.com/Common-Salt-Cell-Questions_b_21.html)). The salt cell is a chlorine generator, not a chlorine substitute. A swimmer with a chlorine sensitivity reacts to salt pool water the same way they'd react to a traditionally chlorinated one, because it's the same molecule doing the sanitizing.

Because of that, CDC's residential pool guidance, at least 1 ppm free chlorine (2 ppm if using cyanuric acid) and pH 7.0 to 7.8, applies identically to salt and traditional chlorine pools ([CDC Healthy Swimming, home pool water treatment and testing](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-swimming/about/home-pool-and-hot-tub-water-treatment-and-testing.html)). Salt system owners still test free chlorine and pH, not just salt level.

## What salt level does a salt pool actually run at?

Most residential salt chlorine generators are designed to operate with the pool water holding 3,000 to 4,000 ppm dissolved salt, roughly a tenth of ocean water's salinity, which is why salt pools feel notably softer than the ocean and most swimmers don't taste salt at all at that concentration. A brand-new salt pool typically needs 40 to 50 lb of pool salt per 2,000 gallons for the initial fill, then only occasional top-offs afterward to replace salt lost to splash-out, backwashing, and dilution from rain ([River Pools, inground saltwater pool cost breakdown](https://www.riverpoolsandspas.com/blog/inground-saltwater-pool-cost)).

Neither is categorically cheaper; the crossover point depends heavily on how many pool seasons you'll own the equipment before the cell needs replacing.

## What maintenance differences actually matter?

Salt water is mildly corrosive to metal that isn't rated for it, ladders, rails, some older heater cores, and certain deck hardware, so retrofitting an existing pool to salt sometimes means replacing components that a traditional chlorine pool never had to touch. Salt cells also need periodic inspection for calcium scale buildup on the plates, which reduces chlorine output if left unaddressed, and most cells have a manufacturer-rated output that declines gradually as the titanium plates wear, not a hard failure point.

Traditional chlorine pools sidestep both of those issues but trade them for a different chore: buying, storing, and handling chlorine chemical containers regularly through the season, plus the cal-hypo storage precautions (cool, dry, away from other chemicals) that come with bulk chlorine purchasing.

## Which is easier day to day?

Salt systems generally win on daily convenience: the generator produces chlorine automatically and continuously as water passes through the cell, so there's less manual dosing and fewer trips hauling chemical buckets. Traditional chlorine gives an owner more direct, immediate control over the exact free-chlorine level, which some pool operators (and most commercial facilities) still prefer for precision, especially during a cleanup where breakpoint dosing needs a specific, fast, controllable chlorine spike that a salt cell's steady output isn't designed to deliver on its own.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a salt water pool actually cheaper than a chlorine pool?**
It depends on the time horizon. Salt systems cost more upfront and add a periodic cell-replacement expense, but cut the recurring cost of buying bagged or liquid chlorine all season. Owners who keep a pool for many years before the cell wears out tend to come out ahead; owners who sell or replaster within a couple of seasons often don't recoup the initial equipment cost.

**Does a salt water pool damage pool equipment?**
It can, specifically metal components not rated for saltwater exposure. Confirm your heater, ladders, rails, and any metal coping or fixtures are salt-compatible before converting an existing chlorine pool, since retrofitting incompatible hardware is a real added cost some owners don't budget for upfront.

**Can any pool be converted to salt water?**
Most inground pools can, since the conversion is mainly adding the salt cell and control board to the existing plumbing plus dissolving salt into the water. Vinyl liner and fiberglass pools generally handle salt fine; older pools with non-salt-rated metal fixtures need those fixtures assessed first.

**How often do you need to add salt to a salt water pool?**
Rarely, after the initial fill. Splash-out, backwashing, and rain dilution gradually lower salt level over a season, so most owners test and top off once or twice a year rather than adding salt on any regular schedule.

Bottom line: a salt pool and a chlorine pool are both chlorine pools; the salt cell just manufactures the sanitizer on-site instead of you buying it pre-made. Choose based on upfront budget, expected years of ownership, and whether your existing equipment is salt-rated, not based on a belief that one is chemical-free and the other isn't.
