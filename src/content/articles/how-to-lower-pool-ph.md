Lower pool pH with muriatic acid (liquid, 31.45% strength) or dry acid (granular sodium bisulfate), added in small doses with a retest between each one, until the reading holds inside the CDC's recommended 7.0 to 7.8 range ([CDC Healthy Swimming, home pool water treatment and testing](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-swimming/about/home-pool-and-hot-tub-water-treatment-and-testing.html)). Most pool professionals aim tighter, 7.4 to 7.6, since that's closest to human tear pH and where chlorine works most efficiently without stinging eyes.

Unlike total alkalinity, which drops in a fairly straight line for every ounce of acid added, pH resists change unevenly because alkalinity buffers it. That's the part most dosing charts skip, and it's the reason a single universal "ounces per point of pH" number doesn't exist the way it does for alkalinity.

## How much acid does it take to lower pH?

There's no single formula, because total alkalinity determines how much a given acid dose moves the pH reading. Water with high alkalinity resists pH change and needs more acid for the same drop; water with low alkalinity swings further on a smaller dose. These are practical starting points, meant to be retested and adjusted, not treated as exact:

| Chemical | Typical starting dose | Effect (in normal 80-120 ppm alkalinity water) |
|---|---|---|
| Muriatic acid (31.45%) | 16 fl oz (1 pint) per 10,000 gal | Lowers pH roughly 0.2 to 0.3 |
| Dry acid (sodium bisulfate) | 15 oz per 10,000 gal (pH 7.6-7.8) | Lowers pH into the 7.4-7.6 range |
| Dry acid (sodium bisulfate) | 20 oz per 10,000 gal (pH 7.8-8.0) | Lowers pH into the 7.4-7.6 range |

Dry acid dosing above reflects a manufacturer's published product label ([In The Swim, pH Reducer product specifications](https://www.intheswim.com/p/ph-reducer/Y7100-VAR.html)); the label caps any single dose at 20 ounces per 10,000 gallons and instructs users to retest before adding more.

## Why doesn't pH move the same amount every time you add the same dose?

Total alkalinity acts as a chemical buffer, absorbing some of the acid's effect before it shows up as a pH change. A pool at 60 ppm alkalinity moves several tenths of a point on a dose that barely nudges a pool sitting at 150 ppm. This is also why total alkalinity is corrected before fine-tuning pH: get alkalinity into its normal 80 to 120 ppm range first, then dose for pH, rather than chasing pH against a moving buffer.

**Worked example:** a 15,000-gallon pool testing pH 7.9 with alkalinity already in range at 100 ppm needs roughly (15,000 ÷ 10,000) × 1 pint = 1.5 pints of muriatic acid to bring pH down toward 7.6, added in one dose, then retested after the water circulates for at least 30 minutes before deciding whether a second, smaller dose is needed.

## Muriatic acid or dry acid: which should you use?

Muriatic acid (liquid hydrochloric acid, typically 31.45% strength) works faster and costs less per treatment, but it's a stronger fume hazard and etches unsealed concrete or plaster if poured in a concentrated stream. Dry acid (sodium bisulfate) is a granular powder that's easier and safer to handle and store, dissolves before it reaches pool surfaces, but costs more per equivalent dose and works slightly slower. As a rough equivalence, about 10 pounds of dry acid produces roughly the same pH and alkalinity effect as 1 gallon of muriatic acid ([In The Swim, pH Reducer product specifications](https://www.intheswim.com/p/ph-reducer/Y7100-VAR.html)).

## How do you add acid to a pool safely?

Always add acid to water, never water to acid; the reaction is exothermic and can splash concentrated acid back at you if done in reverse. Pour muriatic acid in a slow, steady stream across the widest, deepest part of the pool with the pump running, never in one spot near steps, a liner seam, or light fixtures. Pre-dissolve dry acid in a bucket of water before adding it, rather than dumping granules directly into the skimmer or pool.

Wear eye protection and gloves. Never combine acid with chlorine products in the same container, scoop, or feeder; mixing the two is a documented cause of dangerous chlorine gas release and a leading source of the pool-chemical injuries the CDC tracks each year ([CDC, pool chemical safety](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-swimming/toolkit/pool-chemical-safety.html)).

## Why does pH keep drifting back up after you lower it?

Aeration is the usual culprit. Waterfalls, spa jets, fountains, and even ordinary wind and splash on an outdoor pool constantly push carbon dioxide out of the water. As dissolved CO2 leaves, the water's pH rises on its own, independent of anything a swimmer adds. Pools with heavy aeration features or a lot of wind exposure see pH creep upward faster and need more frequent small acid corrections than a calm, sheltered pool of the same size. This is separate from the alkalinity-related pH drift covered elsewhere; even a pool with perfectly balanced alkalinity will see pH climb from aeration alone.

Wait at least 30 minutes with the pump running after any acid dose before retesting; pH takes time to stabilize as the acid fully disperses.

## Frequently asked questions

**What's the fastest way to lower pool pH?**
Muriatic acid works faster than dry acid because it's already dissolved. For a big correction, dose in stages: add a partial amount, run the pump 30 minutes, retest, then add more only if needed. Dumping a large single dose risks overshooting into acidic, corrosive water.

**Can I use vinegar to lower pool pH?**
No. Vinegar is far too dilute to meaningfully shift pH in a pool-sized volume of water, and using enough to matter would introduce more organic material than a pool's chlorine level is designed to handle. Muriatic acid or dry acid are the only practical options.

**Why does my pool pH keep going up on its own?**
Aeration from waterfalls, spa jets, wind, and splash pushes dissolved carbon dioxide out of the water, which raises pH independent of anything added. Pools with heavy water features or high wind exposure need more frequent, smaller acid corrections than calm, sheltered pools.

**How long after adding acid can you swim?**
Most guidance calls for waiting until the water has circulated for at least 30 minutes and the pH has retested inside the 7.0 to 7.8 CDC range before swimming. A large correction dose may need a few hours of circulation before it fully levels out.

Bottom line: lower pH with muriatic acid or dry acid in small, retested doses, correct alkalinity first since it buffers the result, and expect to re-treat more often on aerated or windy pools where CO2 loss keeps pushing pH back up on its own.
