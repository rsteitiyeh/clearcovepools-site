Remove calcium buildup on pool tile by first lowering total alkalinity to 60 to 80 ppm and pH to 7.2 to 7.6, which makes the water aggressive enough to start dissolving the scale, then scrubbing with a wet pumice stone on plaster and concrete or a tile eraser on glazed tile, vinyl, and fiberglass. Stubborn or hard gray deposits usually need a diluted muriatic acid treatment (1 part acid to 10 parts water) applied directly with a brush.

That treatment range, pH 7.2 to 7.6, sits inside the CDC's swim-safe 7.0 to 7.8 band, not outside it ([CDC Healthy Swimming, home pool water treatment and testing](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-swimming/about/home-pool-and-hot-tub-water-treatment-and-testing.html)), so a proper descaling treatment doesn't require closing the pool to swimmers on safety grounds, even though most owners choose to wait until the scale clears.

## What's actually causing the white buildup on your tile?

Calcium scale forms when dissolved calcium comes out of solution and crystallizes on pool surfaces, and it comes in two forms that need different removal methods. Calcium carbonate is the common one: a white, flaky film that builds in layers and generally responds to mechanical scrubbing plus mild acid. Calcium silicate is harder, gray-white, and crystallized, usually the result of high pH and high calcium hardness left uncorrected for a long time, and it typically needs a stronger acid treatment or professional bead blasting to fully clear ([Swim University, how to remove calcium buildup on pool tile, 2026](https://www.swimuniversity.com/remove-calcium-buildup-on-pool-tile/)).

Three things drive scale formation: filling with hard water, letting pH run high for extended periods, and regular use of calcium hypochlorite (cal-hypo) shock, which adds calcium to the water every time it's used.

## Step-by-step: how do you remove it?

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| 1. Test and adjust chemistry | Lower total alkalinity to 60-80 ppm and pH to 7.2-7.6 using muriatic acid or a pH decreaser |
| 2. Brush the tile | Loosen the surface layer with a stiff pool brush before applying any product |
| 3. Apply liquid scale remover | At least a half-gallon per pool; up to two containers for heavy, widespread scale |
| 4. Run the filter 48 hours | Circulates the descaler through the full water volume; avoid swimming during this window |
| 5. Scrub the loosened scale | Wet pumice stone (plaster/concrete) or tile eraser (glazed tile, vinyl, fiberglass) |
| 6. Spot-treat stubborn areas | Apply scale remover directly, let sit a few minutes, scrub again |
| 7. Maintain | Weekly scale-preventer dose; bring TA and pH back to normal range once scale clears |

## What if scrubbing alone doesn't cut it?

For deposits that don't respond to a pumice stone or tile eraser after the chemistry adjustment, mix muriatic acid with water at a 1:10 ratio (one part acid, ten parts water) and apply it directly to the affected tile with a plastic scraper or brush. Let it sit 5 to 10 minutes, or until the deposit visibly starts dissolving, then scrub and rinse the area thoroughly with fresh water to remove any acid residue ([NPT Pool, how to clean pool tile and remove calcium](https://www.nptpool.com/maintenance/how-to-clean-tile-and-remove-calcium/)).

Wear gloves and eye protection for any direct acid application, and always add acid to water, never water to acid. For thick, caked gray scale on plaster or concrete specifically (not tile, fiberglass, or vinyl), a light pass with 80 to 100 grit sandpaper before scrubbing can break up what a pumice stone alone won't touch. If none of this clears the surface, professional bead blasting is the last-resort option for scale that's resisted every chemical approach.

## Which tool for which surface?

Never use a hard pumice stone or sandpaper on glazed tile, vinyl, or fiberglass; both scratch those surfaces permanently. A pumice stone (always used wet, never dry, to avoid scratching) is safe on plaster and unglazed concrete. A dedicated tile or surface eraser, a soft abrasive pad, is the correct tool for anything glazed, painted, or vinyl-lined. When in doubt about a surface's tolerance for abrasion, start with the softer tool and step up only if it doesn't work.

## How do you stop it from coming back?

Keep calcium hardness in its target range: 175 to 225 ppm for vinyl and fiberglass pools, 200 to 275 ppm for concrete and plaster pools. Test pH weekly and calcium hardness monthly, since scale is almost always a sign one of those two drifted high and stayed there. If your fill water is hard, use an inline hose filter or a water softener when topping off or refilling. Switching from cal-hypo shock to dichlor or liquid chlorine removes the calcium-adding side effect that cal-hypo carries with every treatment.

A weekly maintenance dose of a stain-and-scale preventer product won't lower existing calcium hardness, but it keeps dissolved calcium suspended in the water instead of settling back onto tile and plaster.

## Frequently asked questions

**What causes calcium buildup on pool tile?**
High pH, hard fill water, and regular cal-hypo shock use are the three main causes. Calcium stays dissolved at normal pH but crystallizes onto surfaces once pH or calcium hardness climbs too high for it to stay in solution.

**Will vinegar remove calcium deposits from pool tile?**
Vinegar can help with very light, fresh calcium carbonate film in a mixed solution scrub, but it's too weak for established or calcium silicate scale. Muriatic acid diluted 1:10 with water is the reliable option for anything a pumice stone or eraser doesn't clear on its own.

**Is it safe to swim while treating calcium scale?**
The temporary treatment range (pH 7.2-7.6, alkalinity 60-80 ppm) stays inside the CDC's recommended swim-safe pH window, but most owners avoid swimming during the 48-hour filter-circulation period after adding a liquid scale remover since the water chemistry is actively shifting.

**How do you tell calcium carbonate from calcium silicate scale?**
Calcium carbonate looks white and flaky and usually scrubs off with a pumice stone or eraser plus mild acid. Calcium silicate is harder, grayer, and more crystallized; if a pumice stone barely dents it, assume silicate and move to a stronger acid treatment or professional help.

Bottom line: lower alkalinity and pH into the aggressive range, apply a liquid scale remover, then scrub with the tool matched to your surface, stepping up to diluted muriatic acid for anything that doesn't budge. Keep calcium hardness in its target range afterward so the scale doesn't just come back.
