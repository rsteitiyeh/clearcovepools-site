Removing algae from pool walls without damage starts with matching the brush to the surface: nylon bristles for vinyl liner, fiberglass, and painted concrete pools, and stainless steel bristles only for plaster, gunite, or exposed concrete. Brush before shocking to break up the biofilm protecting the algae, working top to bottom in overlapping strokes toward the deep end or main drain, then dose chlorine to breakpoint level and keep brushing daily until no new film returns.

The damage risk isn't from brushing itself, it's from brushing the wrong surface with the wrong bristle. A stiff steel brush that's perfectly safe on gunite will scratch, dull, or tear a vinyl liner or fiberglass gelcoat in a single pass, and that damage doesn't buff out.

## Which brush should you actually use, by surface?

| Pool surface | Correct brush | What happens with the wrong one |
|---|---|---|
| Vinyl liner | Nylon bristle | Wire brush can puncture or tear the liner |
| Fiberglass | Nylon bristle | Wire brush scratches and dulls the gelcoat permanently |
| Painted concrete | Nylon bristle | Wire brush strips and pits the paint layer |
| Plaster / gunite / exposed concrete | Stainless steel or combination bristle | Nylon alone often isn't stiff enough to break embedded algae |

Brushes without a soft bumper edge or rounded corners also raise the risk of gouging fiberglass or vinyl surfaces regardless of bristle type, so check the brush head shape, not just the bristle material, before buying one for a soft-surface pool ([SwimUniversity, best pool brush guide](https://www.swimuniversity.com/pool-brush/)).

## What's the correct brushing technique?

Start at the shallow end and work toward the deep end or main drain, so dislodged algae and debris travel toward the drain rather than getting redistributed across a section you've already cleaned. Brush in a circular or straight overlapping motion, covering each pass with about 50% overlap into the previous stroke, and work top to bottom on walls so debris settles to the floor rather than staying suspended against the wall you just finished ([Florida's Best Pools, brushing pool walls and steps](https://floridasbestpools.com/training/library/pool-cleaning/brushing-pool-walls)). Skipped zones are the most common reason algae seems to "come back" in the same spot repeatedly; it never actually left.

## Do you brush before or after adding chlorine?

Before, always. Algae protects itself with a biofilm layer that chlorine has to penetrate before it can reach living cells; brushing physically breaks that layer apart first, so the chlorine you add afterward works faster and more completely. Once the pool is dosed, keep brushing daily (not just once) until no new film reappears on the walls between brushings, since a single brushing pass rarely removes every colony in one go, especially in corners, steps, and behind ladders.

## What's different about black algae on walls?

Black algae is technically a cyanobacteria, not a true algae, and it behaves very differently from common green algae because it roots into the pores of plaster and gunite surfaces, growing a tough outer layer that resists chlorine on contact. Removing it from a plaster wall requires vigorous, repeated brushing with a stainless steel brush specifically to puncture that protective layer before any chlorine treatment has a chance to work; a single light brushing pass typically isn't enough. Black algae is much rarer on vinyl and fiberglass surfaces, since there are no pores for it to root into, though it can still colonize rough spots, seams, or damaged gelcoat.

## quick reference by algae severity

| Situation | Brush approach | Chlorine approach |
|---|---|---|
| Light green film, early stage | One thorough brushing pass, standard weekly shock | Breakpoint dose to roughly 10x combined chlorine reading ([In The Swim, breakpoint chlorination](https://www.intheswim.com/eguides/breakpoint-chlorination.html)) |
| Established green algae on walls | Daily brushing until clear, 2 to 3 days typical | Breakpoint dose, retest and redose every 12 to 24 hours |
| Black algae spots (plaster/gunite only) | Aggressive repeated brushing to break the protective layer before and during treatment | Breakpoint dose plus a black-algae-specific algaecide per label |

## Frequently asked questions

**Will brushing scratch a fiberglass pool if I use the right brush?**
Not with a nylon-bristle brush designed for fiberglass and reasonable pressure. Fiberglass gelcoat is more delicate than plaster, so avoid excessive force or an abrasive pad even with the correct bristle type; let the chlorine and repeated light brushing do the work rather than scrubbing hard on one spot.

**How often should you brush pool walls during normal maintenance, not just an algae outbreak?**
Weekly, as part of routine care, is enough to prevent algae from establishing a foothold in the first place. Corners, steps, behind ladders, and any low-flow area near returns deserve extra attention, since these are the spots where algae typically starts.

**Can you use a wire brush on a vinyl or fiberglass pool if the algae is really stubborn?**
No, not even for stubborn algae. The liner or gelcoat damage from a wire brush is permanent and typically more expensive to fix than the extra time it takes to clear stubborn algae with repeated nylon brushing and proper chlorine dosing.

**Why do dark stains remain on the wall after the algae itself is dead?**
Dead algae can leave organic or mineral staining behind, especially on plaster, that chlorine alone doesn't lift even once the living algae is gone. Continued brushing during normal maintenance usually fades light staining over time; persistent, dark staining on plaster sometimes needs a dedicated stain treatment or an acid wash, which is a separate, surface-specific job from routine algae removal.
