Clearing a green pool means testing the water, brushing every surface to expose algae hiding in biofilm, dosing chlorine to roughly 10 times the combined chlorine reading (breakpoint chlorination), and running the filter continuously until the reading holds overnight. A light green pool clears in about 24 hours with a single heavy dose; a swampy, algae-choked pool takes 2 to 5 days of repeated dosing and filtering. Professional cleanups run $300 to $800; DIY chemical costs run $50 to $300.

The range is wide because green isn't one problem, it's a spectrum. A pool that's been neglected for a week looks very different from one that sat uncovered and untreated for a month, and the chlorine demand between those two scenarios can differ by a factor of ten.

## Why does a green pool take days to clear instead of hours?

Algae reproduces fast once it establishes: under warm, nutrient-rich conditions, algae cells can replicate every 3 to 6 hours ([Blue Pool Water, Texas green pool cleanup cost data, 2026](https://bluepoolwater.com/blog/green-pool-cleanup-costs-texas/)). Every bit of chlorine added first goes toward satisfying "chlorine demand," the sanitizer consumed neutralizing organic material, dead algae, and bacteria, before any of it becomes a measurable free-chlorine residual. A pool with heavy organic load can absorb an entire shock dose without the test kit showing any free chlorine at all. That's why a single treatment rarely finishes the job: you're not adding chlorine once, you're adding it until the demand is finally satisfied and a residual actually holds.

## What are the exact steps to clear a green pool?

1. **Test first.** Get free chlorine, combined chlorine, pH, and total alkalinity before adding anything; combined chlorine (CC) is the number that sets your breakpoint target.
2. **Brush every surface**, walls, steps, and floor, before dosing. Brushing breaks up the biofilm layer that shields algae cells from chlorine, so treatment that follows works faster.
3. **Get pH into range first.** Chlorine's killing power drops sharply above pH 8.0, so bring pH down toward 7.2 to 7.4 before the big dose; a breakpoint treatment on high-pH water wastes a meaningful share of the chlorine you're adding.
4. **Breakpoint-dose the chlorine.** Target roughly 10 times the combined chlorine reading for a standard bloom, and 20 to 30 times CC for a severe, swampy pool with heavy organic load ([In The Swim, breakpoint chlorination guide](https://www.intheswim.com/eguides/breakpoint-chlorination.html)).
5. **Run the filter 24/7** during cleanup and clean or backwash it far more often than usual; dead algae is fine particulate that clogs cartridges and sand beds quickly.
6. **Vacuum settled algae to waste** (bypassing the filter) if visible debris has settled on the floor, rather than running it through a filter that's already working overtime.
7. **Retest every 12 to 24 hours** and redose if free chlorine hasn't held; a pool that still shows zero free chlorine the next morning still has unmet demand.
8. **Once clear and holding**, drop back to a normal maintenance chlorine level; CDC guidance calls for at least 1 ppm free chlorine at pH 7.0 to 7.8 in residential pools, or 2 ppm if the pool uses cyanuric acid ([CDC Healthy Swimming, home pool water treatment](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-swimming/about/home-pool-and-hot-tub-water-treatment-and-testing.html)).

## How much chlorine does a green pool actually need?

Here's the math worked for a 15,000-gallon pool, using a 65%-available-chlorine cal-hypo shock dosed at the standard rate of 1 lb per 10,000 gallons for roughly a 7.5 ppm rise.

**Light bloom** (combined chlorine tests at 1 ppm, target a 10 ppm rise): (15,000 ÷ 10,000) × (10 ÷ 7.5) × 1 lb ≈ **2 lb** in a single treatment.

**Severe bloom** (combined chlorine tests at 3 ppm, target a 60 to 90 ppm rise at the 20x to 30x multiplier): (15,000 ÷ 10,000) × (75 ÷ 7.5) × 1 lb ≈ **15 lb**, but this is not added as one dose. Manufacturer per-treatment caps and the risk of overwhelming the pool's cyanuric acid balance mean a severe cleanup gets staged across multiple treatments over 2 to 5 days, retesting between each, rather than dumped in all at once.

## How do you know a green pool cleanup is actually finished?

Two signals matter: free chlorine holds steady rather than dropping to zero between tests (meaning demand is finally satisfied, not just temporarily overwhelmed), and the water clears to swimmable visibility once the dead algae is filtered and vacuumed out. Color often clears before clarity does; a pool can shift from green to a hazy blue-gray while cell debris is still working through the filter, which is normal and typically resolves within another day or two of continuous filtration.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can you swim in a pool during a green pool cleanup?**
No. Free chlorine during breakpoint dosing runs far above safe swimming levels, and visibility is usually too poor to see a swimmer at the bottom regardless. Wait until free chlorine has dropped back into the normal 1 to 4 ppm range and the water is clear enough to see the main drain.

**Why does a pool turn green again days after a successful shock?**
Two common causes: the chlorine demand wasn't fully satisfied (a marginal dose knocked the algae back without killing it completely, so it rebounds within days) or cyanuric acid is high enough to be "chlorine locking" the sanitizer, making the free chlorine reading technically adequate but not actually effective. Both call for a retest of CYA alongside chlorine before dosing again.

**Do you need to drain a green pool to clean it?**
Rarely. Most green pools clear with breakpoint chlorination, brushing, and filtration; draining is usually reserved for pools with heavy staining underneath the algae, structural algae mats that won't lift, or water chemistry so far out of range that starting over is faster than correcting it.

**How long until a green pool turns clear blue?**
A light bloom often shifts visibly within 24 hours of the first proper dose. A severe, opaque pool typically needs 3 to 5 days of repeated dosing and constant filtration before it's back to normal swimmable clarity, sometimes longer if the filter itself needs mid-cleanup servicing.

Bottom line: test before you dose, brush before you shock, target roughly 10 times the combined chlorine reading (more for severe blooms), and expect real timelines of 1 to 5 days depending on how far gone the water actually is.
