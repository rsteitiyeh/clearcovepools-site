Cloudy pool water almost always traces back to one of five causes: low or chemically locked chlorine, an out-of-range pH or alkalinity, poor filtration or circulation, an algae bloom, or a heavy bather load. Identify which one it is, correct it, then run the filter continuously for 24 to 48 hours. Reach for a clarifier or flocculant only after chemistry and filtration are both already right, not as a first move.

CDC's pre-swim safety checklist for public pools includes checking that you can see the drain at the bottom of the deep end before getting in ([CDC, preventing swimming-related illnesses](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-swimming/prevention/index.html)). That's a useful home test too. A pool that's merely a little hazy is usually a same-day fix; a pool where the drain has vanished from view needs the full diagnostic before anyone gets back in.

## What actually causes cloudy pool water?

In order of how often each one turns out to be the culprit, according to pool-chemistry sources that see this daily:

1. **Low or "locked" chlorine.** If cyanuric acid (stabilizer) has crept above 80 to 100 ppm, it bonds so tightly with chlorine that sanitizing power drops even when a test strip still reads a normal number, a problem commonly called chlorine lock ([SwimUniversity, cyanuric acid](https://www.swimuniversity.com/cyanuric-acid/)). The standard target range for CYA is 30 to 50 ppm in a chlorine pool, 60 to 80 ppm in a saltwater pool.
2. **Chemical imbalance**, especially high pH or high total alkalinity, which reduces how effectively chlorine can sanitize and lets organic material accumulate.
3. **Poor filtration or circulation**, including a clogged filter, a short daily run time, or dead spots where water barely moves.
4. **Algae bloom**, usually paired with a green or gray tint rather than a plain milky haze.
5. **High bather load and organic contaminants**, sunscreen, sweat, and body oils that combine with chlorine to form chloramines and cloud the water, especially after a pool party weekend.
6. **High calcium hardness or waterline particulates**, which shows up as a persistent haze even when every other chemistry number checks out.

(Ranked causes per [Orenda Technologies' cloudy water diagnostic](https://blog.orendatech.com/cloudy-pool-water) and [PST Pool Supplies' diagnostic guide](https://www.pstpoolsupplies.com/blogs/articles/how-to-diagnose-cloudy-pool-water).)

## How do you tell a filtration problem from a chemistry problem?

Scoop a glass of pool water and hold it up to the light. If the water looks clear in the glass but the pool itself still looks cloudy, the problem is filtration or circulation: the water is fine, it just isn't moving through the filter enough. If the water is still cloudy inside the glass, the problem is chemistry, something in the water itself needs correcting before the filter can catch it ([PST Pool Supplies](https://www.pstpoolsupplies.com/blogs/articles/how-to-diagnose-cloudy-pool-water)).

Test cyanuric acid alongside the standard five numbers (chlorine, pH, alkalinity, calcium, CYA). CYA doesn't show up on a basic test strip in most home kits, and it's one of the most overlooked causes of "I shocked it twice and it's still cloudy."

## What's the fastest way to clear cloudy water?

1. Test and correct pH (7.0 to 7.8) and alkalinity (80 to 120 ppm) first; chlorine can't do its job outside those ranges.
2. If CYA is above 100 ppm, dilute with fresh water; it's the only real fix, since cyanuric acid doesn't break down or evaporate on its own ([PoolWaterLab.us](https://www.poolwaterlab.us/blogs/pool-water-chemistry-explained/cyanuric-acid-cya-pool-safe-levels-testing)).
3. Shock the pool to restore an active chlorine residual.
4. Clean or backwash the filter before running it hard; a dirty filter can't clear cloudy water no matter how long it runs.
5. Run the filter continuously, 24 to 48 hours, instead of the normal 8 to 12 hours a day used for maintenance.
6. If the water is still cloudy after 24 to 48 hours of correct chemistry and continuous filtration, add a clarifier or flocculant.

## Clarifier or flocculant, which one do you need?

| | How it works | Time to clear | Best for | Manual work |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clarifier | Coagulates fine particles so the filter catches them | 2 to 3 days | Mild haze, especially with cartridge filters | None, the filter does it |
| Flocculant | Coagulates particles so they sink to the floor | 1 to 2 days (8 to 16 hours to settle) | Severe cloudiness, fast turnaround | Manual vacuum to waste required |

(Comparison per [SwimUniversity's clarifier](https://www.swimuniversity.com/pool-clarifier/) and [flocculant](https://www.swimuniversity.com/pool-flocculant/) guides, and [Leslie's clarifier vs. flocculant breakdown](https://lesliespool.com/blog/pool-clarifier-vs-flocculant-whats-the-difference.html).)

Flocculant clears water faster but takes more physical work, since it has to be vacuumed out to waste rather than left for the filter to remove. Clarifier is the lower-effort option for water that's hazy rather than severely cloudy, and it works with the filter instead of around it.

## How long does clearing cloudy water actually take?

A mild haze caused by a chemistry imbalance alone often clears within a day of correcting pH and alkalinity and running the filter. A cloudier pool, especially one caused by high CYA or a mild algae bloom, typically takes 2 to 3 days with a clarifier or 1 to 2 days with a flocculant, on top of the time it takes to dial in the underlying chemistry. Water that's still cloudy after a week of correct chemistry and continuous filtration usually means the filter media itself needs cleaning or replacing, not another round of chemicals.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it safe to swim in cloudy pool water?**
If you can't clearly see the bottom of the pool or the main drain, don't swim. That mirrors the same visual check public health guidance uses, and it matters because cloudy water can hide both a chemical imbalance and reduced sanitizer effectiveness at once.

**Can high cyanuric acid cause cloudy water?**
Yes, indirectly. High CYA locks up chlorine's sanitizing power even when the test strip shows a normal reading, which lets organic material and algae get a foothold. The only real fix is diluting the water; CYA doesn't reduce on its own.

**Will a clarifier work if my filter is dirty?**
No. A clarifier coagulates particles specifically so the filter can trap them; if the filter itself is clogged, it can't catch what the clarifier clumps together. Clean or backwash the filter first.

**How long should I run the filter to clear cloudy water?**
Run it continuously, 24 to 48 hours, instead of the usual 8 to 12 hours a day. Cutting the run time short before the water has actually cleared is a common reason a "fixed" pool clouds back up within a day or two.

Bottom line: fix the cause (chemistry, filtration, or bather load) before reaching for a clarifier, and give continuous filtration 24 to 48 hours to do the real work.
